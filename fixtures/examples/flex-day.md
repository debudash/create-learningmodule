## Objectives
- **Content:** Students will draw evidence from informational texts to support analysis in writing, vary sentence patterns for meaning and style, and maintain a formal style and tone in research responses.
- **Language:** Students will explain writing choices using evidence-based language, sentence-combining language, and formal academic wording.
- **Foundational Skills:** Students will reread and revise sentences to check for repetition, clarity, and consistency of tone.

## Essential Questions
- Why were some contributions overlooked in historical accounts, and how can research help us build a fuller record?

- Vocabulary for Additional Support: **style** (the way writing sounds to a reader); **tone** (the writer’s attitude or feeling shown through language); **revise** (to improve writing by changing words or structure)

### Huddle 1 — Using Evidence in Writing (W.6.9.b)

*Use any short passage from* Hidden Figures *or teacher-selected informational text for this huddle. Students should have the text in front of them.*

#### Reteach

- A **claim** is a statement that takes a position or makes a point.
- **Evidence** is a specific quote, detail, or example pulled directly from the text that supports a claim.
- **Commentary** refers to the sentences you write before or after a piece of evidence that explain how or why the evidence proves the claim.

**Say:** In this huddle, we are going to make our writing sound more grounded and convincing. As we look at the text, we will choose one strong detail, connect it to a claim, and explain how that evidence supports the writing.

#### Guided Practice

*Have students reread a short section of the text and identify one detail, fact, or quotation that could support a written point.*

- **Ask:** What is one specific detail, fact, or quote from the text that feels important or strong?
  - Sample response: The strongest detail is the one that explains exactly what the innovator did or what changed because of that work. That detail gives me real evidence instead of a general opinion.

*Have students turn that detail into a clear written point they could make.*

- **Ask:** What claim could that piece of evidence help you prove? What point does it support?
  - Sample response: That detail could help me prove that the innovator made an important contribution. It connects directly to the person’s work, not just to background information.

*Have students draft one sentence that uses commentary to explain the significance of the evidence and how it supports the claim.*

- **Ask:** How would you write a sentence that uses the detail as evidence to support your point? What does the reader need to understand to make a connection between the evidence and your claim?
  - Sample response: In the section describing the project, the source explains that [specific detail], which demonstrates that the innovator’s work had a real effect on the outcome.

#### Quick Check

**Say:** Now you are going to try this on your own. Use the text to write a short claim-evidence-commentary set.

- **Ask:** Pick one claim starter, complete it, and then write 2–3 sentences: your completed claim, a specific quote or detail that supports it, and one sentence of commentary explaining how the evidence proves your claim. Starters:
  - Sample response: The author of [text title] believes _____.
  - Sample response: [Text title] suggests that _____.
  - Sample response: Based on [text title], I would argue that _____.
  - Sample response: The author of [text title] believes [specific idea]. For example, the text states [specific quote or detail], which shows [what the detail reveals about tone, character, or situation]. This demonstrates [the logical connection between the evidence and the argument].

**Integrated ELD Instructions**

> **PURPOSE:**
> Support students in moving from general opinion statements to evidence-based writing that clearly links a source detail to a point.

> **SAY / ASK:**
> Prompt students to point to the exact place in the source that supports their idea before they write.
> Press students to finish the explanation move by adding the phrase which shows that... after the evidence.
> “You said ‘this part proves it’ — we can say: ‘This detail supports my point because it shows...’”
> “You said ‘the text says she helped’ — we can say: ‘In the section describing her work, the source explains that...’”
> One detail that supports my point is...
> In the section describing..., the source explains...
> This detail shows that...
> Invite students to make their claim in everyday language first, then restate it using formal writing language.
> Encourage students to connect the evidence to existing knowledge about the topic.

> **WATCH FOR:**
> If student gives a point without naming a text detail → Prompt: “Show me the exact line or sentence that helped you make that claim.”
> If student copies a detail but does not explain it → Prompt: “Finish this sentence: This detail shows that...”
> Students name a specific detail from the text rather than referring vaguely to the source.
> Students explain how the detail supports the point instead of leaving the evidence to stand alone.

#### Routine: Check for Understanding

> *Circulate and spot-check:*
> - Students select a detail that directly supports a claim.
> - Students articulate a clear connection between the evidence and the claim.
> - Students’ quick-check responses introduce evidence with a clear source reference and commentary explaining its relevance.

**Additional Student Support**

| **Situation** | **Try this** |
| --- | --- |
| **If students need more support with connecting evidence to a claim** | Offer the frame: *“In the section describing ___, the source explains ___. This shows that ___.”* |
| **Ready for extension** | Ask students to add a second piece of evidence and explain how it builds upon or reinforces the claim and evidence they already have. Have students revisit their claim-evidence-commentary set and evaluate whether they chose the strongest possible evidence: “Is there another detail that proves your claim even more directly? Find it, and revise your commentary to explain why the new evidence is a better fit.” |

### Huddle 2 — Varying Sentence Patterns (L.6.3.a)

*Use any short sample passage or sample draft for this huddle. Students should have the text in front of them.*

#### Reteach

- A **sentence pattern** is the way a sentence is built and how it begins or unfolds.
- Writers vary sentence patterns to create emphasis, make ideas clearer, and keep writing from sounding repetitive.
- You can vary a pattern by changing the opening, combining ideas, or adding a phrase that shows time, cause, or contrast.

**Say:** In this huddle, we are going to make writing sound less repetitive and more purposeful. As we work with a sample text, we will notice sentence patterns, name one way to change them, and revise a sentence so it carries the idea more effectively.

#### Guided Practice

*Have students look at two nearby sentences from the text and notice how the sentences begin and sound.*

- **Ask:** What do you notice about how these sentences are built?
  - Sample response: I notice that both sentences start in a similar way, so the writing sounds repetitive. The ideas are clear, but the pattern does not change much.

*Have students identify one revision move that would change the pattern while keeping the meaning.*

- **Ask:** What is one way you could change the sentence pattern to make the writing clearer or more effective?
  - Sample response: I could start one sentence with a time phrase or combine two connected ideas into one sentence. That would make the writing flow better and highlight the important idea.

*Have students rewrite one sentence about their topic using a different opening or structure.*

- **Ask:** How would you revise one sentence so it has a different pattern but keeps the same meaning?
  - Sample response: Instead of starting with the subject again, I could write, “By solving [problem], [innovator] helped move the project forward.” That keeps the meaning but changes the pattern.

#### Quick Check

**Say:** Now you will revise a sample of your own writing so it sounds smoother and more deliberate. Keep the meaning clear, but change the pattern.

- **Ask:** Look for two sentences that feel repetitive or lack variety in length or style. Write one revised sentence or two varied sentences.
  - Sample response: [Innovator] changed people’s minds. When others doubted that the project could succeed, [innovator] stepped in to provide a solution.

**Integrated ELD Instructions**

> **PURPOSE:**
> Support students in using different sentence openings and structures to create clearer, more effective academic writing.

> **SAY / ASK:**
> Model how to reread sentences aloud and listen for repetition in beginnings or rhythm.
> Prompt students to name the effect of the revision, not just the change itself, by asking how the new pattern improves meaning or flow.
> “You said ‘it sounds the same’ — we can say: ‘The sentence pattern is repetitive and needs variation.’”
> “You said ‘I changed the start’ — we can say: ‘I varied the sentence opening to create emphasis and smoother flow.’”
> I notice that both sentences...
> One way to vary the pattern is...
> This revision improves the writing because...
> Invite students to orally test two versions of a sentence and choose the one that sounds clearer.
> Encourage students to use examples of sentences from familiar stories as a bridge to stronger written sentence variety.

> **WATCH FOR:**
> If student changes words but not the sentence pattern → Prompt: “Did the sentence actually begin or unfold in a new way, or did only one word change?”
> If student revises into a confusing run-on → Prompt: “Say the new sentence aloud. Where does one idea end and the next begin?”
> Students identify repetition in sentence beginnings or structure.
> Students revise a sentence pattern to improve flow without altering meaning.

#### Routine: Check for Understanding

> *Circulate and spot-check:*
> - Students notice when sentences sound repetitive.
> - Students revise using a genuinely different sentence pattern.
> - Students' quick-check responses preserve the original meaning while improving style.

**Additional Student Support**

| **Situation** | **Try this** |
| --- | --- |
| **If students need more support with changing sentence openings** | Offer the stems: *“By ___, [individual]...”* *“Because of [individual], [event and details]” or “After ___, [individual]...”*<br>Let students say the revised sentence aloud before writing it, or record the sentence orally and then copy the best version. |
| **Ready for extension** | Challenge students to write two different revisions and explain which one creates stronger emphasis. |

### Huddle 3 — Maintaining Formal Style (L.6.3.b)

*Use any short passage or sample draft for this huddle. Students should have the text in front of them.*

#### Reteach

- **Formal style** uses precise, appropriate language for school and research writing.
- **Tone** should stay consistent across a response so the writing sounds focused and serious.
- Writers can revise informal words into clearer academic language.

**Say:** In this huddle, we are going to make writing sound more polished and appropriate for research. As we work, we will spot informal wording, replace it with stronger academic language, and revise a sentence so the tone stays formal from beginning to end.

#### Guided Practice

*Have students look at a sentence from a sample draft or provide a sample sentence like “[Innovator] did a lot of really cool stuff, and people should totally know about it.” Prompt them to find any word or phrase that sounds too casual for research writing.*

- **Ask:** Which words or phrases sound too informal for an informative response?
  - Sample response: Words like “a lot,” “cool,” or “stuff” sound too informal because they are vague and conversational. A research response should sound more precise.

*Have students replace one informal phrase with a more formal and accurate one.*

- **Ask:** What formal word or phrase could replace that wording?
  - Sample response: I could replace “a lot of important stuff” with “an important contribution” or “significant work.” That sounds more specific and formal.

*Have students revise the full sentence so the whole tone is formal and consistent.*

- **Ask:** How would you revise the whole sentence so it sounds formal from beginning to end?
  - Sample response: I would revise the sentence to say that the innovator made a significant contribution that deserves recognition. That version sounds more appropriate for research writing.

#### Quick Check

**Say:** Now you will revise a casual sentence so it matches the tone of an informative research response. Keep the meaning, but change the wording so it sounds formal and consistent.

- **Ask:** Select a piece of your own writing to review and look for language that uses a casual tone. Revise the wording to use a formal tone, and explain why you made the changes you did.
  - Sample response: [Revised sentence]. I changed the phrase “really awesome” to “incredibly remarkable” because it explains how interesting and important this person is, and keeps the tone as formal as the rest of my paragraph.

**Integrated ELD Instructions**

> **PURPOSE:**
> Support students in revising casual language into precise academic wording so their writing matches the demands of research tasks.

> **SAY / ASK:**
> Prompt students to name exactly which words sound too casual instead of saying only that the sentence sounds off.
> Model how to revise vague phrases into precise nouns and verbs connected to the unit, such as contribution, recognition, advanced, or significant.
> “You said ‘that sounds too casual’ — we can say: ‘The style is too informal for a research response.’”
> “You said ‘she did cool stuff’ — we can say: ‘She made a significant contribution to the field.’”
> This wording is too informal because...
> A more formal way to say that is...
> This revision creates a more consistent tone because...
> Honor student language awareness by inviting comparison between how they might say the idea in conversation and how they would present it in a formal school setting.
> Encourage students to use strong academic words they have already learned.

> **WATCH FOR:**
> If student revises only one word but the sentence still sounds casual → Prompt: “Read the whole sentence again. Does every part match a formal research voice?”
> If student chooses a formal word that changes the meaning too much → Prompt: “Does your new word still say the same idea more precisely, or did the meaning shift?”
> Students identify vague or casual language that weakens tone.
> Students revise the full sentence so the style remains formal and consistent from start to finish.

#### Routine: Check for Understanding

> *Circulate and spot-check:*
> - Students identify at least one informal word or phrase accurately.
> - Students replace vague wording with a more precise academic phrase.
> - Students’ quick-check responses maintain a formal tone throughout the sentence.

**Additional Student Support**

| **Situation** | **Try this** |
| --- | --- |
| **If students need more support with formal word choice** | Offer a quick replacement list: *cool → significant*, *stuff → work* or *contribution*, *a lot → many* or *substantial*. |
| **Ready for extension** | Ask students to revise additional sentences of their own writing and explain how the new tone better matches an informative article. Challenge students to combine formal tone with a varied sentence pattern in one polished revision. |
