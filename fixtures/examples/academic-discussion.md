## Objectives
- **Content:** Students will use a Fishbowl Conversation to make connections between the novel A Single Shard, the article “The Youngsters Keeping Traditional Trades Alive,” and the stages of learning.
- **Language:** Students will synthesize ideas across A Single Shard, “The Youngsters Keeping Traditional Trades Alive,” and the stages of learning by using evidence-based discussion stems, synthesis phrases (e.g., across both texts, taken together), and clear oral explanations that cite where evidence appears.
- **Foundational Skills:** Students will cite and synthesize evidence from multiple texts during structured academic discussion.

## Section 2: Literacy Lab (10 min)

**Purpose:**

Students will construct a list of conversation norms for their Fishbowl Conversation.

### Conversation Norms

*Lead students in a co-construction of the conversation norms they will be following during today’s group discussion. You may follow the sequence below, or, if time permits, allow students to identify and name norms using a less structured format.*

*Display each identified norm for students to see during discussion.*

**Directions:** Work together to develop conversation norms for the text-based group discussion.

- **Say:** Our first norm will focus on listening.
- **Ask:** Why is it important to listen during a discussion?
  - Sample response: An effective discussion has a balance between listening and speaking, giving everyone a chance to share their ideas and taking time to think about others’ points before responding.
- **Say:** We can call this norm "Listen without interrupting.” This means that group members take turns, one voice at a time, waiting until a speaker finishes before responding.
- **Say:** Our second norm will be about using evidence to support ideas.
- **Ask:** Why is it important to use evidence to support your ideas?
  - Sample response: When discussing a text, you don’t just share ideas; you support them with details from a text or other source so that people know where your ideas came from.
- **Say:** This norm can be: “Support ideas with evidence.” When you provide evidence, include a page number or describe the excerpt so others can find it. This way, others can see where your ideas came from.
- **Say:** The next norm focuses on using language from the text to express our ideas.
- **Ask:** Why is it important to use language from the text?
  - Sample response: Using specific language from the text to explain your ideas gives a speaker credibility and also keeps the discussion grounded in the text.
- **Say:** This norm can be: “Use language from the text.” This includes referencing specific names of people, places, and concepts in fiction, as well as using domain-specific or technical vocabulary in informational texts.
- **Say:** Our final norm should focus on how we respond: in a strong discussion we address ideas rather than individuals, using both a respectful tone and language.
- **Ask:** Why do you think it’s important to respond to ideas rather than individuals?
  - Sample response: If you react to individuals, they might take it personally. Responding to ideas keeps the discussion on the text and people won’t feel as though they are being criticized if you disagree with their ideas.
- **Say:** Let’s write the norm, “Respond to ideas rather than individuals.” Instead of labeling a comment as “wrong,” we explain our thinking using evidence.

**Teacher Tip**

> Encourage students to use their Unit Vocabulary graphic organizers or Personal Dictionaries to support domain-specific language use during the **Fishbowl Conversation**.

### Part A: Preparing for Discussion (SL.6.1, SL.6.4) (10 minutes)

### Preparing for Fishbowl Conversation

*Provide students with Accountable Talk sentence stems and Reflect-and-Respond graphic organizers to support their participation in the discussion. Explain that the sentence stems can guide them in agreeing, disagreeing, and building on others’ ideas, while the Reflect-and-Respond graphic organizer can be used to record questions, ideas, and observations before, during, and after the discussion.*

*Before students prepare for their group discussion, explain the* ***Fishbowl Conversation*** *routine.*

- **Directions:** During the discussion today, you’re going to follow the **Fishbowl Conversation** routine. This routine helps us build meaning together instead of just sharing separate opinions. Here’s how it works:
  - The class will be split into two groups: one will sit in a circle in the middle of the room (the inner circle), and the other will sit in a circle around the first group (the outer circle).
  - Students in the inner circle will have 10 minutes to discuss the question prompts.
  - While the inner circle group is discussing, the outer circle group will silently take notes on the inner circle’s discussion to add to their own thinking and provide responses to points brought up by the inner circle.
  - After 10 minutes, the groups will switch places, and the new inner circle group will have their turn to discuss the question prompts while the new outer circle group silently takes notes.

*Provide students with the question prompts they will be discussing during the Fishbowl Conversation. Then model a response to at least one of the question prompts using discussion stems and examples (see the sample responses below).*

- **Ask:** How does an apprentice navigate the stages of learning in order to succeed in a craft? Use textual evidence to support your ideas.
  - Sample response: An apprentice navigates the stages of learning by seeking out opportunities to observe and to try. For example, the article explains how leatherworking apprentice Leighann Perry became interested in her trade after observing leatherworking at a museum and then looked for a way to learn the skill.
- **Ask:** Across Chapters 1–8 of *A Single Shard*, what patterns show how observation, trying, and failing help Tree-ear grow as a learner? Which moments best demonstrate how difficult learning actually works? Use textual evidence to support your ideas.
  - Sample response: Chapters 1–8 show how observing, trying, and failing are a part of a cycle. For example, Tree-ear observes other potters cutting clay from the riverbank. He tries to replicate their actions and instead gets his spade stuck. He tries again and eventually succeeds. Once he has learned this skill, he observes, tries, and fails at other parts of the pottery-making process, such as purifying the clay to the exact right consistency.
- **Ask:** What does it take to learn something difficult? Cite examples from the novel and the article to support your ideas..
  - Sample response: Both the novel and the article show that learning something difficult takes perseverance and risk. In *A Single Shard*, Tree-ear takes risks by asking Min for an apprenticeship and continues to work hard even when he struggles or things don’t go his way. Min perseveres and takes risks when he tries, fails, and works to perfect the technique developed by Kang. In the article, the globe-making apprentice Eddy da Silva explains how he took a risk to pursue his interest: “The 24-year-old, who was born in Venezuela but now lives in England’s capital, left a corporate environment to pursue the craft but said ‘only a handful of people supported my decision.’”

**Teacher Tip**

> Invite students to stop you and ask questions as you model the procedure. Highlight and discuss when/why/how you use citing/reporting verbs (*demonstrates*, *requires*, *shows*, *suggests*) and cause–and–effect language (*because*, *so that*) to support your points. Tell students that during their discussion, they should focus on grounding their ideas and responses in textual and visual evidence from both texts. Students can use the Accountable Talk Sentence Stems graphic organizer for examples of discussion stems they can use during the Fishbowl Conversation.

#### Routine: Pulse Check (RI 6.1, RL 6.1)

> Which statement best captures what both texts suggest about learning a challenging task?
> A. Learning a trade depends mostly on natural talent.
>    - Incorrect: The texts show that success comes from practice and perseverance, not natural talent alone.
> B. Apprentices must work independently, without guidance from others.
>    - Incorrect: Both texts emphasize mentorship and guidance, not learning alone.

**Integrated ELD Instructions**

> **PURPOSE:**
> Help students prepare Fishbowl Conversation contributions by organizing claims and evidence from both texts and rehearsing synthesis language before discussion begins.

> **SAY / ASK:**
> Encourage students to use their own learning experiences as a quick “hook,” then pivot to textual proof (“That’s similar to Tree-ear when . . .”).
> Have students annotate their notes with N (novel) and A (article) to ensure balance.
> Prompt students to select “best evidence” (not the longest).
> Convert summaries into claims (“This shows that learning requires . . .”).
> Model synthesis phrasing: “Taken together, these sources suggest . . .”
> Encourage precise verbs: shows, demonstrates, suggests, reveals.
> My claim is ___. In *A Single Shard* (p. __), ___ shows ___. In the article, ___ shows ___. Taken together, this suggests ___.
> One pattern across both texts is ___.
> A key difference is ___, which matters because ___.
> Provide sentence frames for multilingual learners based on their proficiency levels as appropriate, and gradually reduce these supports as students become independent.
> **Emerging:** “In the novel, ___ shows ___.” / “In the article, ___ shows ___.”
> **Expanding:** “My claim is ___. According to A Single Shard (p. __), ___, and the article shows ___.”
> **Bridging:** “My claim is ___. In A Single Shard (p. __), ___ shows ___; in the article, ___ shows ___. Taken together, this suggests ___.”

> **WATCH FOR:**
> Require a simple prep structure: claim → evidence (novel) → evidence (article) → synthesis sentence.
> If students have evidence from only one text → Prompt: Add one supporting detail from the other text.
> If students have facts only → Prompt: Add an interpretation sentence using “This suggests . . .”
> Students prepare at least one claim supported by two sources.
> Students use one synthesis phrase accurately.
> Students include location language (page/section).
> Students at different proficiency levels may need to begin at different points within the task. Based on responses, look for opportunities to scaffold up or release supports.
> **Emerging:** Prepares one claim with one piece of evidence from each text using a frame.
> **Expanding:** Prepares a claim supported by both texts and rehearses one synthesis phrase aloud.
> **Bridging:** Prepares a precise claim with cited evidence from both texts and uses location language plus a synthesis phrase.

### Part B: Fishbowl Conversation (SL.6.1, SL.6.4) (20 minutes)

#### Routine: Fishbowl Conversation

**Directions:** Now we will engage in the **Fishbowl Conversation** routine using the agreed upon norms. Remember to refer to your **Accountable Talk Sentence Stems** to help you phrase your questions and comments. Also use your **Reflect-and-Respond** graphic organizer to record questions and ideas as they come up during the discussion.

*Divide students into the inner circle and outer circle groups. After 10 minutes, have the groups switch places so that all students have the chance to contribute to the discussion.*

**Additional Student Support: Fishbowl Conversations**

| **Situation** | **Try this** |
| --- | --- |
| **Struggling with: Inner circle discussion** | Give a simple two-step routine that keeps students focused on stating a claim and offering evidence from one or both texts. Step 1: State a claim. Ex. “I noticed that the article makes the point ____.” Ex. “I believe an important point is that ____.” Step 2: Prove the claim with details from the texts. Ex. “The article says ____, which shows ____.” Ex. “This connects to *A Single Shard* when ____ (p. __) because ____.” Remind students to face the inner circle, speak at a conversational volume, and pause briefly after their claim to ensure the outer circle can track the evidence. |
| **Struggling with: Outer circle note-taking** | Give a two-step routine that helps students capture what was said and what evidence supports it. Step 1: Track the conversation. Ex. “____ argued that ____, using the article ____ (section __)/*A Single Shard* (p. __).” Step 2: Prepare one response to share when circles switch. Organize your response so you first acknowledge the speaker’s point, then present your evidence in a sequence that builds on what they said. Ex. “I heard someone say ____, and I can add ____ from the article (section __)/*A Single Shard* (p. __).” |
| **Ready for extension: Making connections** | **Ask:** How are the depictions of the stages of learning in *A Single Shard* similar to and different from the way they are shown in the article “The Youngsters Keeping Traditional Trades Alive”? I noticed that both the novel and the article describe the experiences of young people learning a new skill and give insight into what they’re thinking and feeling during the process. For example, in *A Single Shard*, the author includes narration about Tree-ear observing, trying, failing, and refining, as well as details about his thoughts and feelings as he learns to make pottery. In the article, the writer gives firsthand accounts from apprentices about their experiences. |

**Integrated ELD Instructions**

> **PURPOSE:**
> Support students in sustaining an evidence-based Fishbowl Conversation that synthesizes ideas across texts rather than producing disconnected comments.

> **SAY / ASK:**
> Encourage students who speak less to enter with a prepared stem and one evidence card (short, planned contribution).
> Inner circle: require each turn to include a claim and at least one cited detail.
> Outer circle: require notes that capture claim and evidence and one follow-up question.
> Midway switch: prompt new speakers to reference something previously said (“Building on . . .”).
> Prompt synthesis moves: connect two speakers (“That relates to ___’s point because . . .”).
> Upgrade vague agreement: “I agree, and the evidence that strengthens it is . . .”
> Recast personal opinion into text-grounded reasoning.
> Building on ___’s idea, the novel/article shows ___.
> A detail that supports this is ___ (p. __ / article quote).
> Taken together, these examples show ___.
> Can you point to where the text suggests that?
> Provide sentence frames for multilingual learners based on their proficiency levels as appropriate, and gradually reduce these supports as students become independent.
> **Emerging:** “The novel/article shows ___.” / “I agree with ___ because ___.”
> **Expanding:** “Building on ___’s idea, the novel/article shows ___ (p. __), which suggests ___.”
> **Bridging:** “Building on ___’s idea, A Single Shard shows ___ (p. __) and the article shows ___; taken together, these examples reveal ___.”

> **WATCH FOR:**
> If students drift into personal stories only → Prompt: Connect back to a text moment.
> If students quote without explanation → Prompt: Add “This shows . . .” and your claim.
> Students reference both texts across the discussion (not just one).
> Students ask at least one clarifying/probing question.
> Students build on a peer’s idea with evidence.
> Students at different proficiency levels may need to begin at different points within the task. Based on responses, look for opportunities to scaffold up or release supports.
> **Emerging:** Contributes one turn using a stem; cites one detail from a named text.
> **Expanding:** Builds on a peer’s idea with brief reasoning and cites a specific moment from at least one text.
> **Bridging:** Synthesizes peers’ ideas across both texts, cites specific moments with location language, and uses a synthesis phrase to draw a warrant.

#### Routine: Checklist

> You will turn in your discussion notes for feedback. A strong draft should:
> Use accurate and relevant evidence from both texts, including where the evidence can be found.
> Explain the relationship between the stages of learning, how they help learners acquire difficult skills, and how they are represented in the texts.
> Explain relationships between events/ideas using domain-specific vocabulary and temporal connectors.
> Build on others’ ideas using Accountable Talk Sentence Stems while keeping comments relevant to the conversation and texts.
> Present claims and findings using appropriate eye contact, adequate volume, and clear pronunciation to ensure the audience can follow their reasoning.
