## Objectives
- **Content:** Students will plan an informative research essay about one hidden innovator’s key contribution and plan an argument paragraph about why the innovator deserves recognition.
- **Language:** Students will use subordinate clauses (because, although), cohesive previewing language, and precise academic verbs to combine a claim with a reason or counterpoint and draft a clear thesis statement.

## Essential Questions
- How do curiosity, evidence, and collaboration lead to discovery?
- How can research help us uncover lesser-known contributions and tell a more complete story?

## Section 1: Launch (5 min)

**Purpose:**

Reintroduce the full performance task and connect finalized research to planning the informative research essay and argument paragraph.

*Use this launch to reconnect students to the Performance Task and emphasize that strong research writing explains a meaningful contribution using clear evidence and explanation. Reinforce that planning is essential before drafting. It requires identifying a clear focus, selecting relevant evidence, and thinking about why the work matters.*

***Reintroduce the Prompt:***

*For this task, you will write a research essay, an argument paragraph, and present your learning. Your essay will explain one key contribution made by a hidden innovator—someone whose work was important but not widely recognized. You will use evidence from multiple sources to explain their work, the context they worked in, and why their contribution matters.*

*You will also write a short argument explaining why your innovator deserves recognition. Then, you will present your ideas to the class, using evidence and visuals to support your thinking. In this lesson, we will begin planning your research by identifying your innovator and their key contribution.*

***Explain the Significance of the Task:***

- **Say:** Not all important contributions are recognized. Learning about hidden innovators helps us better understand how progress happens and whose work has been overlooked.

***Explain the Criteria for Success:***

*Review the criteria for success in the handout to set the expectations.*

- **Ask:** How will you show success in your writing based on these criteria?
  - Sample response: I will show success by using evidence from multiple sources and explaining how it proves the innovator’s contribution matters.

### Connection to Today's Learning

- **Say:** Now that you understand the expectations for our performance task, you are ready to begin by learning how to build a thesis that can guide both the essay and the argument paragraph.

## Section 2: Literacy Lab (10 min)

**Purpose:**

Use the Sentence Combining routine to work on a thesis statement. Practice joining a simple claim with a reason or a counterpoint using words like *because* and *although* to create a powerful, compelling argument.

*Students learn to transform disconnected research notes into a unified thesis statement. The focus is on combining ideas using subordinate clauses to show contrast and causation, preparing students for both informative and argumentative writing structures.*

#### Routine: Sentence Combining

Display and read: Mary Jackson wanted to be an engineer.

- **Say:** This sentence names a topic, but it is not yet a thesis. A thesis needs to show a claim and explain why it matters.

***Kernel Sentences***

#### Display:

- Mary Jackson wanted to be an engineer.
- She faced barriers.
- Her work made a difference at NASA.

### Teach: Combining Ideas into a Thesis

*In this lesson, we combine short ideas into one strong thesis sentence. We use although to show a challenge and because to explain why the idea matters.*

### Model and Deconstruct

*Display Combined Sentence:*

- Although Mary Jackson faced barriers, her engineering work matters because it changed how NASA studied flight problems.
  - **Ask:** What word introduces the challenge in the sentence? Sample response: (although)
  - **Ask:** What part of the sentence states the main claim? Sample response: (her engineering work matters)
  - **Ask:** What word introduces the reason the work matters? Sample response: (because)
  - **Ask:** What does the part after *because* explain? Sample response: (It explains why her work is important.)
  - **Ask:** How does combining these ideas make the sentence stronger? Sample response: (It connects the challenge, the claim, and the reason in one clear sentence.)

Sample response: To build a strong thesis, we:

- Sample response: combine ideas instead of listing them
- Sample response: use *although* to show a challenge or contrast
- use *because* to explain significance
- check that the subject and verb agree

*This helps the sentence sound clear, formal, and ready for an essay.*

**Directions:** In your journal, turn your own research notes into one thesis sentence. Use *because* to connect your claim to a reason, or use *although* to connect your claim to a challenge or contrast. Then reread your sentence and check that your subject and verb match.

### Connection to Today’s Learning

- **Say:** You created a clear thesis sentence that connects ideas and explains why your topic matters. Next, you will organize your notes so each part of your essay supports that claim.

**Integrated ELD Instructions**

> **PURPOSE:**
> Students use sentence combining to build formal thesis statements that connect a hidden innovator’s contribution to its significance.
> **Language Focus:**
> Subordinate clauses with because and although
> Subject-verb alignment in combined sentences
> Formal thesis language for research writing
> Precise verbs including matters, changed, enabled, improved
> Before Sentence Combining (preparation step—optional)
> Students say three short ideas aloud: the innovator, the contribution, and why it mattered.

> **SAY / ASK:**
> Prompt students to begin with clear notes before combining: who, what, and why it matters.
> Remind students to reread the finished sentence aloud to hear whether the subject and verb sound right together.
> "You said 'She did something important'—we can say: 'Her contribution mattered because it changed the field in a specific way.'"
> "You said 'Even though people ignored her'—we can say: 'Although her work was overlooked, her contribution still shaped later discoveries.'"
> Although ___ faced ___, their work matters because ___.
> ___ made an important contribution because ___.
> My thesis shows significance by explaining ___.
> Invite students to rehearse their sentence in a home language or everyday English first and then work together to shift it into a more formal school sentence.
> Encourage students to use the sentence pattern as a bridge from how they naturally explain ideas aloud to how they present ideas in academic writing.

> **WATCH FOR:**
> If students write only a topic sentence with no reason → Prompt: "Add because and tell why the contribution mattered."
> If students create a long sentence that loses its main idea → Prompt: "Stop after your main claim. Then add just one reason that best supports it."
> If students mismatch the subject and verb in the thesis → Prompt: "Point to the subject first. Is it one thing or more than one? Now choose the verb that matches."
> Student combines at least two related ideas into one clear thesis statement.
> Student names a contribution and a reason for significance without losing subject-verb alignment.

**Additional Student Support**

| **Situation** | **Try this** |
| --- | --- |
| **Struggling with: Combining Ideas into One Sentence** | Give students three quick note stems: “___ contributed ___,” “___ faced ___,” and “This mattered because ___.” Using Subordination: Provide a partial frame such as “Although ___, ___ matters because ___.” Subject-Verb Alignment: Have students underline the subject once and circle the verb before rereading the sentence aloud. Written Expression: Allow students to say the full thesis aloud first, record it orally, or use speech-to-text before copying the final version. |
| **Ready for extension** | Invite students to draft two thesis statements about their innovator—one using because and one using although—and decide which one better fits the direction of their informative research essay. |

## Section 3: Learning in Action (30 min)

**Purpose:**

**Part A: Plan the Informative Research Essay (W.6.2, W.6.5)** Students will use Research Notes and the Research Essay Outline to plan an introduction, body sections, evidence placement, and at least one visual that supports explanation. **Learning in Action B: Plan the Recognition Argument Paragraph (W.6.1, W.6.5)**

Students will use the Argumentative Essay organizer to plan a short argument paragraph and orally walk through how both organizers fit together.

### Part A: Plan the Informative Research Essay (W.6.2, W.6.5) (20 minutes)

*This section helps students move from notes to structure. Model how to sort evidence into the Research Essay Outline so the writing will be coherent and evidence-based.*

#### Teach: Review Rubric

*Reintroduce the rubric:*

- **Say:** I have lots of notes, but I cannot just place them randomly into my outline. First, I look at my thesis statement and ask what section of the essay it promises to explain. Then I sort my notes by those sections. One note helps explain background, another shows the key contribution, and a third shows the impact of that contribution. I also notice that a timeline would fit best in the section about impact because it helps show when the change happened. That means my outline is not just a list of facts; it is an organized plan that helps the reader follow my explanation. I refer to the rubric to ensure my outline will help me meet the final expectations.
- **Ask:** Which section of the essay should hold your strongest evidence about the innovator’s key contribution, and why?
  - Sample response: The strongest evidence about the key contribution should go in the middle body section because that is where I explain what the innovator actually did. That section needs the clearest facts, examples, and possibly a visual that helps the reader understand the contribution.

**Directions:** Use your Research Notes and begin filling in your Research Essay Outline. Write your thesis statement, plan your body sections, and place the strongest facts from at least two sources under the section where they fit best.

**Integrated ELD Instructions**

> **PURPOSE:**
> Students organize research notes into a logical essay structure and use planning language to connect evidence to sections of an informative research essay.
> **Language Focus:**
> Logical sequencing language
> Academic previewing structures
> Categorizing language for evidence and visuals
> Formal informative writing register
> Before Modeled Writing (Preparation Step—Optional)
> Students quickly label three notes as background, contribution, or impact before placing them in the outline.

> **SAY / ASK:**
> Prompt students to group related notes rather than list facts in the order they found them.
> Encourage students to place visuals where they clarify explanation, not as decoration.
> This section will explain ___.
> One fact that belongs here is ___.
> This visual supports my explanation of ___.
> My research shows that ___ contributed to ___.

> **WATCH FOR:**
> If students place facts randomly → Prompt: “Ask what job the fact does in the essay: background, contribution, impact, or significance.”
> If students separate visuals from explanation → Prompt: “Add the visual where it best clarifies a fact or process.”
> If students overload one section with all their notes → Prompt: “Keep the strongest evidence and move extra details to a section where they fit better.”
> Student places notes into logical sections of the outline.
> Student identifies where a chart, graph, statistic, or diagram may strengthen explanation.

**Additional Student Support**

| **Situation** | **Try this** |
| --- | --- |
| **Struggling with: Logical Sequencing** | Suggest the pattern background → contribution → impact → significance. Organizational Confusion: Remind students that the informative research essay explains, while the argument paragraph makes the case for recognition. Visual Placement: Ask which section would be clearer if the audience could see a chart, graph, diagram, or timeline. Alternative Expression: Allow students to orally explain where a note belongs before writing it into the outline. |
| **Ready for extension** | Ask students to add a note about where they may use a statistic or chart to strengthen the impact section. |

### Part B: Plan the Recognition Argument Paragraph (W.6.1, W.6.5) (10 minutes)

*Students shift from explanation to persuasion by organizing a recognition argument that introduces a clear claim about why an innovator deserves recognition, supports that claim with relevant reasons and evidence, and uses transitions to connect ideas and clarify relationships between claims and evidence. Students maintain a formal academic style while explaining the significance of the innovator’s contributions and conclude with a statement that reinforces why recognition of overlooked contributions matters.*

*Informative Research essay = explains contribution*

*Recognition Argument paragraph = justifies recognition*

*Review the criteria specifically for the recognition argument paragraph in the Performance Task handout.*

*Explain how to write a clear claim.*

*A clear claim can also use subordination:*

- Because ___, this innovator deserves recognition.
- Although ___, this innovator merits acknowledgment because ___.
- **Say:** My informative research essay explains the contribution, but my argument paragraph is a separate piece of writing that answers a different question. I am no longer just explaining what happened. I am making the case that this person deserves recognition. So I need a claim, reasons, and evidence that match that purpose. Because Dorothy Vaughan’s leadership and programming work enabled NASA to adapt to major technological change, she deserves recognition as a key contributor whose work was long undervalued. That claim uses the same research, but it shifts into an argument.

*Argumentative writing must stay organized around the claim. Writers introduce the claim first, then organize reasons and evidence so each detail supports the same central idea.*

#### Display:

*Claim → Reason → Evidence → Explanation → Concluding Statement*

- **Say:** If my paragraph jumps randomly between ideas, my argument becomes confusing. Strong argument writing organizes every sentence around proving the claim.

### Think- Pair-Share

**Directions:** In your Argumentative Essay organizer, write a claim about why your innovator deserves recognition. Then add one or two reasons and the strongest evidence that supports the claim. After that, discuss your response to the question below.

- **Ask:** What is your recognition claim, and how does it connect to your informative research essay plan?
  - Sample response: My claim is that my innovator deserves recognition because her contribution transformed the field and opened opportunities for others. It connects to my informative research essay because the essay explains the contribution in detail, while the argument paragraph uses that same research to explain why she merits acknowledgment.

*As students conclude, they should be able to answer the guiding question: How does your argument paragraph connect to your essay plan?*

**Integrated ELD Instructions**

> **PURPOSE:**
> Students distinguish between informative and argumentative purposes and organize a short argument paragraph that grows from their research.
> **Language Focus:**
> Claim language
> Subordinate clauses in argument claims
> Cohesive planning language
> Justification language including deserves recognition, merit, acknowledge, honor
> Before Think-Pair-Share (preparation step—optional)
> Students say their argument claim aloud once before writing it into the organizer.

> **SAY / ASK:**
> Prompt students to make a claim, not just repeat the thesis from the essay.
> Encourage students to explain how the argument paragraph depends on research evidence from the essay plan.
> Because ___, this innovator deserves recognition.
> Although ___, their work merits acknowledgment because ___.
> This contribution matters because ___.
> My essay explains ___, and my argument paragraph shows ___.

> **WATCH FOR:**
> If students copy the informative thesis into the argument claim → Prompt: “Add deserves recognition or merits acknowledgment so the paragraph has a persuasive purpose.”
> If students give weak reasons → Prompt: “Link the reason to impact on science, society, or belonging in STEM.”
> If students blur the two organizers together → Prompt: “Ask which organizer explains and which organizer argues.”
> Student writes a distinct claim for the argument paragraph.
> Student explains the relationship between the essay outline and the argument.

### Check for Understanding

*Provide students with a confidence continuum (i.e., 1–5). As needed, model how to demonstrate a level of confidence using the continuum.*

**Reflection (W.6.1)**

> Reflect on your ability to write an argument using the **Reflection** routine.
> - How confident are you in stating a claim based on evidence?

**Additional Student Support**

| **Situation** | **Try this** |
| --- | --- |
| **Struggling with: Recognition Claim Formation** | Provide a simple claim stem with because already included. Separating Essay and Argument Purposes: Ask students to say one sentence: My essay explains ___. My argument proves ___. Reason Development: Prompt students to connect reasons to impact, fairness, or belonging in STEM. Alternative Expression: Allow students to orally walk through both organizers before writing final entries. |
| **Ready for extension** | Invite students to add a possible counterargument and a brief response in the organizer. |

## Section 4: Look Back (5 min)

**Purpose:**

Students will reflect on the strength of their thesis and organization and set a next step for completing the outline.

*Students reflect on readiness to draft by evaluating the clarity of the thesis, the organization of notes, and the strength of the argument claim. This reflection reinforces the transition from planning to writing.*

#### Routine: Quick Write

**Directions:** Write your response to the question.

- **Ask:** How ready are your writing plans for drafting, and what still needs work?
  - Sample response: My essay plan is ready because I have a thesis, clear body sections, and an argument claim about recognition. One strength is that my evidence is sorted by contribution and impact. My next step is to finish the outline and make sure each section has the strongest paraphrased facts and a useful visual where needed. My paragraph plan is ready because I have evidence for my claim.

Sample response: Optional Sentence Starter:

Sample response: *My essay outline is strong because ___. My next step is ___.*
