## Objectives
- **Content:** Students will explain how Katherine Johnson’s mathematical evidence and collaboration led to safer flight regulations using transitions to clarify relationships among ideas.
- **Language:** Students will use sequencing transition words and cause/effect connectors to organize paraphrased evidence in an explanatory paragraph.
- **Foundational Skills:** Students will use formal causal transitions to signal cause/effect relationships across ideas.

## Section 2: Literacy Lab (10 min)

**Purpose:**

Use a mentor sentence from Chapter 13 to teach how explanatory writers show sequence and cause/effect with precise transitions.

*Students will learn how explanatory writers show cause-and-effect relationships using precise transitions to connect ideas.*

#### Routine: Language Study

**Teach:** ***Showing Cause and Effect with Transitions***

**Directions:** In this lesson, we will study how writers connect ideas by showing cause-and-effect relationships. Instead of just listing events, writers show how one idea leads to another using precise transitions to explain the connection. Read and review the following quotes from Chapter 13 then respond to the questions..

***Model and Deconstruct***

*Read the first quote aloud and model responses to the questions.*

*"The data showed that the air disturbance caused by a jet that flew past could trouble the air for as long as a half hour after it had passed through."*

- **Ask:** What event or action is the cause in the first sentence? Sample response: (the air disturbance caused by a jet)
- **Ask:** What is the effect? Sample response: (the air could be troubled for up to half an hour)
- **Ask:** Which words signal the cause-and-effect relationship? Sample response: (caused by)

*Prompt students to work with a partner to repeat the process you modeled using the second quote from Chapter 13.*

*"The research done by Katherine and the engineers on the team led to changes in air traffic regulations, requiring minimum distances between flight paths to prevent similar accidents."*

- **Ask:** What is the cause in the second sentence? Sample response: (the research done by Katherine and the team)
- **Ask:** What is the effect? Sample response: (changes in air traffic regulations)
- **Ask:** Which words show the relationship between the ideas? Sample response: (led to)

*Lead a whole class discussion of the following question:*

- **Ask:** How do these transitions help the reader understand the sentences? Sample response: (They show how one idea directly results in another.)

Sample response: To show cause and effect, writers:

- Sample response: identify what happened (cause)
- Sample response: explain what happened next (effect)
- use transitions like *caused by, led to*, and *as a result*

*This helps readers understand how ideas are connected, not just what happened.*

**Connection to Today’s Learning**

- **Say:** In this lesson, you explored how cause-and-effect language can be used to connect ideas. Next, you will rehearse and write how each step in Katherine’s investigation led to the next and how her mathematical work and collaboration resulted in safer flight.

**Integrated ELD Instructions**

> **PURPOSE:**
> Support students in interpreting how explanatory writing uses transitions to show cause-and-effect relationships and sequence across ideas.
> **Language Focus:**
> Cause-effect transitions (caused by, led to, as a result)
> Sequencing transitions (first, next, eventually)
> Noun phrases that name scientific causes and outcomes

> **SAY / ASK:**
> Students whisper-read the mentor sentence and identify the phrase that names the cause and the phrase that names the effect.
> Emphasize that phrases like “data showed” ground explanations in evidence rather than opinion.
> Point out that transitions such as “led to” clearly connect cause and effect and help organize explanation.
> You said the data told them something—we can say the data revealed the cause of the disturbance.
> You said the research helped—we can say the research led to changes in air traffic regulations.
> The data showed that ___ caused ___.
> ___ led to ___.
> As a result, ___.
> Invite students to orally paraphrase the mentor sentence in everyday language before revising it into formal academic language.

> **WATCH FOR:**
> If students list events without showing connection → Prompt: “What caused this to happen? What happened as a result?”
> If students overuse simple connectors like then → Prompt: “Replace then with a more precise transition like ‘led to’ or ‘as a result.’”
> If students copy the sentence exactly → Prompt: “Say the idea in your own words first, then write it.”
> Student identifies the cause and effect in a sentence.
> Student explains how a transition signals the relationship between ideas.
> Student describes how transitions help connect ideas clearly.

| --- | --- |

| **Ready for extension** | Challenge students to revise a sentence by replacing a simple connector with a more precise transition such as consequently or as a result. Ask students to explain how changing the transition changes the clarity of the relationship. |

## Section 3: Learning in Action (30 min)

**Purpose:**

**Part A: Use Transitions Effectively (W.6.2.c)**

Students will organize the sequence of Katherine’s investigation using appropriate transitions to clarify the

relationships among ideas and concepts.

**Part B: Draft the Explanation (W.6.2.c)**

Students will write an explanatory paragraph using appropriate transitions to show how Katherine’s mathematical evidence and collaboration led to safer flight.

### Part A: Use Transitions Effectively (W.6.2.c) (15 minutes)

**Directions:** With a partner, review the Chapter 13 section “A Bumpy Ride.” Work together to map the investigation steps from the initial problem to the resulting flight safety change. Identify each step, paraphrase the details that describe it and use transitions to show how the process moves forward. Pay close attention to the cause-and-effect relationships. Take notes in a 3-Column Chart and label each column as indicated:

- Column 1: Step
- Column 2: Paraphrased evidence
- Column 3: Transition word or phrase

- **Ask:** What is the first step in Katherine’s investigation and how does it launch the additional steps that eventually lead to safer flight regulations?
  - Sample response: The first step was when Katherine analyzed the flight recorder images and compared them to the later experiment data. That is where the evidence begins to show that rough air from the larger jet stayed dangerous after the jet had already passed. Without that step, the later rule change would not make sense.

**Integrated ELD Instructions**

> **PURPOSE:**
> Support students in orally sequencing the investigation and explaining how each stage led to the next.
> **Language Focus:**
> Process explanation language
> Paraphrasing scientific actions
> Cause/effect connectors

> **SAY / ASK:**
> Prompt students to use text landmarks such as “In the part where Katherine studies the images” rather than vague references like “in the book.”
> Listen for whether students can explain both the investigation step and its consequence.
> You said she looked at the recorder—we can say she analyzed the flight recorder images in small increments of time.
> You said they tested it—we can say the engineers recreated the conditions to confirm what the evidence suggested.
> In the part where Katherine ___, the evidence began to show ___.
> Next, the engineers ___ to test ___.
> Eventually, this investigation led to ___.
> Encourage students to rehearse the process with hand motions for each stage: problem, analysis, experiment, result.

> **WATCH FOR:**
> If students retell the crash but not the investigation → Prompt: Move to what the scientists did after the crash. What evidence did they study?
> If students explain steps but not collaboration → Prompt: Add who worked with Katherine and what that team action contributed.
> Student sequences the investigation in logical order.
> Student explains at least one link between evidence and outcome using a transition.

**Additional Student Support**

| **Situation** | **Try this** |
| --- | --- |
| **Struggling with: Moving from Notes to Explanation** | Provide this oral rehearsal frame: First, ___. Next, Katherine used evidence to ___. Then, the team ___. As a result, ___. |
| **Ready for extension** | Ask students to add one sentence explaining why the new regulations mattered beyond this single crash. |

#### Routine: Pulse Check (W.6.2.c)

> Which sentence best explains a cause/effect relationship in Katherine’s investigation?
> A. Katherine looked at the images, then the engineers did an experiment, and then the rules changed.
> - Incorrect: This choice lists events but does not clearly explain how one step caused the next.
> B. Katherine analyzed the flight recorder images, and as a result, the team had evidence to test and could identify the hidden danger in the air.
> - **Correct: This sentence clearly connects Katherine’s analysis to the team’s next step and shows how evidence moved the investigation forward.**
> C. Katherine worked hard, and the chapter is interesting because the investigation was important.
> - Incorrect: This choice is too general and does not explain the process or the causal link between steps.
> D. The crash happened after a jet passed through the area, and Katherine liked the work.
> - Incorrect: This choice includes details from the section, but it does not explain how the investigation led to safer flight.

### Part B: Draft an Explanation (W.6.2.c) (15 minutes)

*Students will draft an explanatory paragraph using their notes from Part A. Remind them that this is not a summary of the whole chapter; it is an explanation of the steps that led to safer flight regulations.*

#### Routine: Quick Write

#### Teach:

- **Say:** As I draft an explanatory paragraph, I start with the big idea, not with a tiny detail. My topic sentence answers the question by naming both mathematical evidence and collaboration. Then I move in order: Katherine’s analysis first, the experiment next, the discovery after that, and the safety change at the end. I am careful to paraphrase, which means I keep the meaning but use my own wording. I also underline my transitions so I can check whether the paragraph sounds smooth instead of repetitive. Before I finish, I reread and ask, Did I show how each step led to the next one?

**Directions:** Use your notes in the 3-Column Chart to write an explanatory paragraph that responds to this question: How did Katherine’s mathematical expertise and collaboration with others lead to safer flight?

Include in your paragraph:

- at least two paraphrased details from the section
- transitional language to show the sequence and cause/effect relationships,
- a final sentence that explains the resulting safety impact.

Underline your transitions as you write.

- Sample response: Katherine Johnson’s mathematical evidence and collaboration helped make flight safer by revealing the hidden cause of a crash. First, she carefully analyzed the flight recorder images from the small plane so the team could study what happened over time. Next, the engineers recreated the same conditions with a test flight, and Katherine compared that new information to the earlier evidence. The two kinds of evidence pointed to the same conclusion. As a result, the team learned that disturbed air from a larger jet could remain dangerous long after the jet had passed. Eventually, this discovery led to changes in air traffic regulations that required more distance between flight paths. Katherine’s analysis and the team’s experiment worked together to help prevent similar accidents.

**Integrated ELD Instructions**

> **PURPOSE:**
> Support students in producing informative writing with a clear structure, transitions, and paraphrased evidence.
> **Language Focus:**
> Topic sentences for explanation
> Paraphrased evidence
> Formal transitions for sequencing and result
> Before Drafting (preparation step—optional)
> Students orally rehearse just their first two sentences with a partner before writing.

> **SAY / ASK:**
> Confer first with students whose drafts begin as summaries and help them restate the writing question into a focused topic sentence.
> Point students back to the sequence notes if they skip from problem to result without explaining the middle steps.
> You said this made things safer—we can say this discovery led to changes in air traffic regulations.
> You said they worked together—we can say Katherine’s analysis and the engineers’ experiment confirmed the same conclusion.
> Katherine’s evidence mattered because ___.
> After Katherine analyzed ___, the team ___.
> This discovery led to safer flight because ___.
> Invite students to orally compose a sentence in everyday language first and then revise it into formal school language with a partner.

> **WATCH FOR:**
> If students copy the text word for word → Prompt: Close the book and tell me the idea in your own words before you write it.
> If students use many transitions but the logic is still unclear → Prompt: Stop after each sentence and answer: What did this step lead to?
> Student uses transitions to show a logical sequence across the paragraph.
> Student explains how evidence and collaboration contributed to the discovery, not just what happened.

Sustaining the Explanation: Invite students to draft one sentence at a time and orally rehearse each sentence before writing it. |
| **Ready for extension** | Challenge students to replace a basic word like showed or changed with a more precise academic word such as revealed or resulted in. |
