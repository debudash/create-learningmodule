## Section 1: Launch (5 min)

**Purpose:**

Students will activate prior knowledge from Lessons 1–3 and connect the idea of hidden figures to central ideas related to curiosity and community support.

*Pair students with the same partner they used in Lesson 3 if possible so they can quickly build from prior thinking.*

#### Routine: Turn and Talk

**Directions:** Turn and talk to discuss your response to the following question.

- **Ask:** What possibilities emerge when an individual’s curiosity is recognized and nurtured by their community?
  - Sample response: A person’s curiosity can grow into real skill when other people, especially those in their community, support it. For example, if a student loves math and teachers and family encourage that interest, the student may begin to imagine a future in science or engineering.

**Connection to Today’s Learning**

- **Say:** We are going to analyze how the author uses details in the Prologue and Chapter 1 to develop central ideas. When writing about these sections, focus on how the text presents not only the barriers the young women faced but also the support offered by families, schools, and communities that helped them pursue their goals.

**Teacher Tip**

> The Prologue and Chapter 1 include references to segregation and exclusion. Emphasize how the text explores both the significant barriers faced by Black women as well as the supportive role of families, schools, and communities in helping them achieve their goals.

## Section 2: Literacy Lab (10 min)

**Purpose:**

Students will use morphology and reference tools to determine the meanings of key domain-specific words that connect to central ideas in the text.

#### Routine: Introduce New Words Using Morphology

***Target Words:*** *aeronautics, regulations*

**Directions:** Today we’re learning about the domain specific vocabulary *aeronautics* and *regulations*. Let’s explore these words more closely.

Introduce the Word: Present the word *aeronautics* to students and pronounce it.

- **Ask:** Have you seen the word *aeronautics* before? Where?

Identify the Root: Underline the root *aero* in *aeronautics*. Explain that *aero* comes from Greek and means “air.”

- **Ask:** Do you know any other words with the root *aero*? Sample response: (*aerospace, aeroso*l)
- Language Connection: Connect to *aerial* and *aerobic* (both relate to air).

Identify Affixes: Circle *naut* and *-ics* in *aeronautics*. Explain that *naut* means “sailor” or “navigator,” and *-ics* refers to a field of study or science.

- **Ask:** What do you think *naut* might mean based on words you know such as astronaut? Sample response: (someone who travels or navigates)
- Language Connection: Connect to Spanish *aeronáutica*, which has a similar meaning.

*Determine Meaning:*

- **Ask:** Using what we know about *aero*, *naut*, and *-ics*, what do you think *aeronautics* means? Sample response: (the science or study of traveling through the air, like flying aircraft)

*Repeat the routine with regulations:*

Introduce the Word: Present the word *regulations* to students and pronounce it.

- **Ask:** Have you seen the word *regulations* before? Where?

Identify the Root: Underline the root *reg* in *regulations*. Explain that *reg* comes from Latin *regere*, meaning “to rule” or “to guide.”

- **Ask:** Do you know any other words with the root *reg*? Sample response: (*regulate, regular, region*)
- Language Connection: Connect to *regal* and *reign* (both relate to ruling or control).

Identify Affixes: Circle *-ulate* and *-ions* in *regulations*. Explain that *-ulate* means “to control or make,” and *-ions* shows it is a noun meaning “the act or result.”

- **Ask:** What do you think *-ions* might tell us about the word? Sample response: (that it’s something people do or a system)
- Language Connection: Connect to Spanish *regulación/regulaciones*, which has a similar meaning.

*Determine Meaning:*

- **Ask:** Using what we know about *reg*, *-ulate*, and *-ions*, what do you think *regulations* means? Sample response: (rules or laws that control how something is done)

**Integrated ELD Instructions**

> **PURPOSE:**
> Support students in using morphology and reference tools to build precise word meaning and use those meanings to understand the chapter’s ideas.
> Language Focus:
> morphology language
> domain-specific STEM vocabulary
> precise definition frames

> **SAY / ASK:**
> Students say each word aloud with a partner and notice which syllables sound most familiar.
> Prompt students to name both the word part and the context clue: What in the sentence helped confirm your guess?
> If students copy a full dictionary entry, ask them to restate the meaning in their own words.
> You said “it’s about planes” — we can explain that by saying “aeronautics is the scientific field that studies flight.”
> That idea connects to regulations because regulations are official rules that shape what people are allowed to do.
> I think ___ means ___ because the part ___ suggests ___.
> The sentence confirms my thinking because ___.
> After I verified the word, I revised my meaning to ___.
> Allow students to connect -ics words they already know from school or home languages, such as mathematics or physics, to build transfer across word families.

> **WATCH FOR:**
> If students focus only on one word part and ignore the sentence → Prompt: “What does the whole sentence tell you about how this word is being used here?”
> If students confuse a topic word with a general feeling word → Prompt: “Is this word naming a field, a rule, or a description?”
> Student explains meaning using both morphology and context.
> Student revises a preliminary meaning after checking a reference source.

#### Routine: Check for Understanding (L.6.4.b)

> List the words *aeronautics* and *regulations* in your **Personal Dictionary**. Underline the base or root word in each and circle each prefix and suffix. After each word, 1) record notes about the meanings of each word part, and 2) write the definition of the word.

## Section 3: Learning in Action (30 min)

**Purpose:**

**Part A: Track the Big Idea (RI.6.2)**

Students will reread sections of the Prologue and Chapter 1, determine a central idea and locate supporting details.

**Part B: Explain the Big Idea (RI.6.2)**

Students will write a short evidence-based explanation using transitions and complex sentences.

### Part A: Track the Big Idea (RI.6.2) (15 minutes)

*Direct students to reread the part of the Prologue that introduces the women’s love of math and science, and the section of Chapter 1 that describes Black schools, churches, families, and community life.*

#### Routine: Close Read & Annotation Protocol

**Teach:** *Distinguishing Details from Central Idea.*

- **Say:** In Chapter 1, I notice the details that show how Black families, churches, and schools created strong learning environments despite segregation. Details alone can’t form a central idea, but together with an explanation, they can help reveal what the author is showing: curiosity can grow into something powerful, especially when a community supports and nurtures it.

**Directions:** Access the Prologue and Chapter 1 and label your 3-column chart as indicated below.

- Column 1: Key details about curiosity
- Column 2: Key details about community
- Column 3: Central ideas

Use the three‑column chart to record key details from the Prologue and Chapter 1 that relate to (1) curiosity (2) community. Then identify a central idea that these details help develop. After listing the details in the first two columns, use your own words in the third column to explain what each detail shows.

**Ask:** How does the author convey the women’s curiosity about math and science?

- Sample response: In the prologue section, the author shows that the women were drawn to math and science and wanted to understand difficult problems.

**Ask:** What detail in Chapter 1 shows community support for learning?

- Sample response: In Chapter 1 when the author describes Black families, schools, and churches building strong learning spaces, the text shows that the community expected children to study hard and keep growing.

**Ask:** What central idea do those details develop together?

- Sample response: Together, these details develop the central idea that Black communities nurtured the curiosity and ambition of women pursuing careers in science and math and supported their professional goals despite the many challenges they faced.

**Ask:** What does Shetterly seem to want readers to understand about Black women in science that might otherwise be overlooked? Use evidence from the text to support your thinking.

- Sample response: Shetterly seems to want readers to understand that Black women were serious, capable scientists whose contributions were ignored because of racism and sexism. She includes details about community support and curiosity to show that these women were not exceptions and that they were part of a larger story of Black achievement.

**Integrated ELD Instructions**

> **PURPOSE:**
> Support students in distinguishing between a detail and a central idea and in explaining how multiple details connect to one larger point.

> **SAY / ASK:**
> If students retell the chapter, prompt them with: What bigger point do these details build?
> Encourage students to use the word develops in their oral responses: The author develops this idea by...
> You said “the girls were smart” — we can say: “the author develops the idea that their curiosity and talent were strengthened by community support.”
> That idea connects to the central idea because it brings several details together under one bigger point.
> One central idea is ___.
> The author develops this idea by showing ___.
> This detail matters because it suggests ___.
> Allow students to first explain the connection between details in everyday language with a partner, then restate it using the academic frame One central idea is...

> **WATCH FOR:**
> If students confuse a personal reaction with analysis → Prompt: “Tell me what the author is showing, not how you feel about it yet.”
> If students list two details without linking them → Prompt: “What is the common thread connecting these details?”
> Student names a central idea broad enough to include multiple details.
> Student paraphrases evidence instead of copying isolated words without explanation.

#### Routine: Pulse Check (RI.6.2)

> Which statement best captures a central idea developed across the prologue and Chapter 1?
> A. The women’s success happened quickly once adults noticed they were talented.
>    - Incorrect: This choice reflects the misconception that support removed barriers right away. The text shows support mattered, but unfair limits still existed.
> B. Curiosity mattered, but only individual talent shaped the women’s future.
>    - Incorrect: This response ignores the author’s emphasis on schools, churches, families, and community structures that nurtured talent.
> C. Curiosity, drive, and community support helped Black women to excel in the science and math fields.
>    - **Correct: This answer captures the broader central idea about the power of scientific interest, strong community support, and motivation.**
> D. The main point of these sections is that airplane science became more advanced in the early 1900s.
>    - Incorrect: This detail is about science and history, but it is too narrow and doesn’t connect the women’s experiences.

### Part B: Explain the Big Idea (RI.6.2) (15 minutes)

### Teach:

- **Say:** Writers write an explanatory paragraph by starting with a clear central idea, then explaining how key details from a text support that idea. By using transitions like “for example” or “this suggests” writers create clear connections between the ideas and the supporting evidence. For explanatory writing, it’s important to avoid opinion language and to focus only on how the evidence develops the idea. When possible, use domain specific language from the text to develop your ideas.

*Display the following writing model if needed for support and guidance:*

*One central idea in the prologue and Chapter 1 is that curiosity about math and science started opening new possibilities for Black women because their communities helped that curiosity grow. In the part of Chapter 1 that describes Black schools, families, and churches, the author shows that adults built strong support systems for learning even under segregation. For example, the text explains that education was protected and valued in Black communities, which helped talented girls continue developing their skills. As a result, their interest in subjects like math and science began to challenge the false idea that Black women did not belong in scientific fields.*

#### Routine: Quick Write

**Directions:** Use your notes to write a 3–4 sentence paragraph that explains a central idea from the prologue and Chapter 1 of *Hidden Figures.*

Begin your paragraph with “One central idea from the text is…” and use key details to support your explanation. Incorporate sentence stems such as “This detail shows…” or “This evidence conveys…” to connect your evidence to the central idea.

When possible, include domain-specific language from the text to strengthen your explanation.

**Integrated ELD Instructions**

> **PURPOSE:**
> Support students in producing an evidence-based explanation that links paraphrased details back to a central idea with clear transitions and academic register.

> **SAY / ASK:**
> If students drop in a detail without explanation, prompt: Finish the sentence “This detail shows...”
> If students use opinion language, redirect with: Replace “I think” or “I like” with “The author shows” or “The text suggests.”
> You said “this part is important” — we can say: “this detail develops the central idea by showing how support made further learning possible.”
> That idea connects to as a result because you are explaining what happened after the support or curiosity was described.
> One central idea is ___ because ___.
> For example, the text states ___.
> This detail shows ___, which suggests ___.
> Invite students to rehearse their paragraph idea aloud in a shared home language first, then restate it in English using the evidence frames.

> **WATCH FOR:**
> If students quote or paraphrase without linking back to the idea → Prompt: “Add one more sentence that begins with ‘This detail shows...’”
> If students write two unrelated details→ Prompt: “Which bigger idea do both details fit under?”
> Student states a central idea before listing evidence.
> Student uses a transition or subordinating conjunction to connect evidence and explanation.

#### Routine: Reflection (RI.6.2)

> **Reflection** : Reflect on how confident you are in determining the central idea from key details in a text?
> Choose a number between 1 and 5, with 1 being the least confident and 5 being the most confident, to rate your confidence level. Then write a sentence or two describing how confident you are with identifying the central idea and key details in a text.
> **Modeling:**
> **Say:** I am definitely improving in my ability to find the central idea in a text and I would give myself a 3 in the department, but sometimes it is still hard for me to find the best details to support that idea. I think I would give myself a 4 for that.

**Additional Student Support: Evidence-Based Writing**

| **Situation** | **Try this** |
| --- | --- |
| **Struggling with connecting evidence to explanation:** | Provide this frame: In the section where ___, the author shows ___, which suggests ___. writing the paragraph independently: Allow students to orally record their explanation first or use speech-to-text before copying or revising a final version. |
| **Ready for extension** | Ask students to add a final sentence explaining how the text begins to challenge racial or professional boundaries. Have students revise one sentence to strengthen cohesion by adding although, when, or as a result. |
