## SECTION 2: Literacy Lab  ([10, 10] min)
[PURPOSE]
  Students will learn how to turn a written claim into a clear oral statement that explains what the texts reveal and guides the audience through their thinking.
_This mini-lesson teaches students how to present their essay and visual clearly by stating a claim, explaining evidence, and guiding the audience through their ideas using academic language._
[ROUTINE icon=notes_section] Language Study
_In this lesson, we study how presenters use their writing to guide an audience. A strong discussion contribution follows a clear path: claim, evidence, explanation, visual, and connection to others’ ideas. Instead of reading everything, presenters explain what matters so the audience can follow their thinking._
- Weak presentation: This is my essay. Both texts are about courage. This is my chart.
- Strong presentation:
First, both _The Lightning Thief_ and my myth show that challenges test a hero’s courage. For example, each character faces danger that forces them to make difficult choices. This shows that courage develops through struggle. This visual shows how each text presents that idea differently. Ultimately, these stories reveal what people value about courage.
- **Ask:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  What is missing from the weak presentation?}
  - {SAMPLE-RESPONSE: It does not explain the ideas or connect them.}
- **Ask:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  What is the first thing the strong presentation does?}
  - {SAMPLE-RESPONSE: It starts with a clear claim.}
- **Ask:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  How is evidence introduced?}
  - {SAMPLE-RESPONSE: It uses For example to give evidence.}
- **Ask:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  What does the phrase }{TEACHER-SCRIPT: _This shows_}{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  do?}
  - {SAMPLE-RESPONSE: It explains what the evidence means.}
- **Ask:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  How does the presenter explain the visual?}
  - {SAMPLE-RESPONSE: They say what the visual shows and how it connects to the idea.}
- **Ask:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  Why is this better than reading the essay out loud?}
  - {SAMPLE-RESPONSE: It helps the audience understand the ideas step by step.}
To meet the expectations of your presentation, we:
- state a clear claim
- use evidence from our essay
- explain what the evidence shows
- explain how our visual supports the idea
- guide the audience with transitions
- build on or respond to another speaker’s idea
_This helps the audience understand our thinking._
**Say these Directions:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  Using your own essay and visual, rehearse:}
- {TEACHER-SCRIPT: Sentence 1: your claim}
- {TEACHER-SCRIPT: Sentence 2: your evidence (use For example)}
- {TEACHER-SCRIPT: Sentence 3: your explanation (use This shows)}
- {TEACHER-SCRIPT: Sentence 4: your visual explanation (use This visual shows)}
- {TEACHER-SCRIPT: Sentence 5: a connection to another idea (use This connects to… or In contrast…)}
{TEACHER-SCRIPT: Do not read your essay. Explain your ideas in your own words.}
- **Ask:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  What makes a presenter meet the expectations for this task?}
  - {SAMPLE-RESPONSE: They clearly explain their claim, use evidence, explain what it shows, and connect the visual to their idea.explains the evidence, and tells the audience what the visual helps them understand.}
**Connection to Today’s Learning:**
- **Say:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  In this lesson, you practiced presenting your ideas clearly using your essay and visual. Next, you will use these same moves in your discussion to share your claim, explain your evidence, and respond to others.}

## SECTION 3: Learning in Action  ([30, 30] min)
[PURPOSE]
  **Part A: Rehearse the Presentation (SL.6.4, L.6.3.a)**
  Students will prepare and refine oral delivery using discussion moves, presentation frames, and peer feedback.
  **Learning in Action B: Present and Respond (SL.6.4, SL.6.6)**
  Students will participate in a seminar discussion, present evidence, paraphrase peers, and build on ideas.
### **Part A: Rehearse the Presentation (SL.6.4, L.6.3.a) (10 minutes)**
[ROUTINE icon=graphic_organizer] Rehearse and Refine
_Students work in triads with essays and visuals visible. One student speaks, one listens for evidence and clarity, and one listens for sentence variety and audience-friendly delivery; then roles rotate._
**Say these Directions:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  We are going to rehearse for the seminar discussion so your ideas are clear, and you are ready to respond to others and build on their thinking. As you practice, use your essay as a support, not a script, and make sure you can explain how your visual supports your claim. You have two minutes to present.}
##### **Teach:**
- **Say:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  When I enter the discussion, I begin with my claim instead of with a long plot summary. Then I name one text moment, such as in Percy’s return home or in the scene where Odysseus recognizes Ithaca, so listeners know where my evidence comes from. After that, I explain what my visual shows and why it matters. When I respond to a peer, I first paraphrase their idea, then I build on it or offer a contrast using evidence.}
_Display the following Discussion Moves:_
- Enter discussion: According to my essay . . .
- Build on a peer: Building on ___’s idea . . .
- Synthesize across myths: Across these myths, a recurring pattern emerges . . .
- _Connect to modern values: This reflects shared human concerns with ___._
[COLLAPSIBLE "Integrated ELD Instructions"]
  [CALLOUT variant=purpose]
    Support students in preparing for seminar or podcast participation by rehearsing claim language, evidence citation, paraphrasing, and audience-aware oral delivery.
    **Language Focus:**
    Paraphrasing a peer
    Citing evidence orally
    Building and extending ideas
    Adapting tone for an academic audience
    Before Rehearsal
    Students highlight one phrase in their essay they can say exactly and one sentence they should paraphrase into more natural oral language.
  [CALLOUT variant=say]
    Remind students that oral presentation can sound more natural than essay writing while still remaining formal and precise.
    Coach listeners to respond first with paraphrase, then with feedback, so speaking stays collaborative rather than competitive.
    You said “My visual is about the hero choices.” We can clarify and say “My visual highlights how the hero’s choices shape identity across both texts.”
    You said “I agree with him.” We can expand that and say “I agree with his point that courage develops through choice, and my evidence adds . . .”
    In the section where __________, the text reveals __________.
    If you look at my visual, you can see __________, which supports my claim that __________.
    What I hear you saying is __________, and I would add that __________.
    Let students practice pronunciation of names, places, and culture-specific terms with a peer before full-group speaking.
    Encourage students to draw on prior seminar language from Lesson 36 and earlier comparison writing from Lessons 29, 32, and 35. Provide sentence frames for multilingual learners based on their proficiency levels as appropriate, and gradually reduce these supports as students become independent. **Emerging: **What I hear you saying is ___. **Expanding: **In the section where ___, the text reveals ___. **Bridging: **What I hear you saying is ___, and I would add that in the section where ___, the text reveals ___, which supports my claim that ___.
  [CALLOUT variant=watch]
    If students hesitate after a peer speaks because they are unsure how to respond → Prompt: “Start with ‘What I hear you saying is . . .’ and then add one connection from your own essay.”
    If students rely only on written wording and sound overly scripted → Prompt: “Keep the idea, but say it in the way you would explain it to a smart listener who has not read your essay.”
    Student names a text landmark and connects it to a claim.
    Student paraphrases a peer accurately before responding rather than repeating or changing the original idea. Students at different proficiency levels may need to begin at different points within the task. Based on responses, look for opportunities to scaffold up or release supports. **Emerging: **Paraphrases a peer's idea before responding. **Expanding: **Cites a text landmark and links it to a claim while paraphrasing a peer. **Bridging: **Synthesizes a peer's idea with their own evidence and explanation, adapted for an academic audience.

### **Part B: Present and Respond (SL.6.4, SL.6.6) (20 minutes)**
_Keep presentations brief and focused. Each presentation should last about two minutes. If time does not allow every student to present today, continue presentations during the next class session._
##### **Reflect and Response Dialogue**
During this discussion, your job is to do four things:
- present one clear claim from your essay,
- explain how your visual supports that claim,
- respond to at least one peer by paraphrasing their idea,
- and build on or challenge that idea using evidence or a comparison.
_**Questions for Preparation and Delivery**_
**Say these Directions:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  Think about these questions to prepare for the presentations}
- {TEACHER-SCRIPT: Based on your comparison of }{TEACHER-SCRIPT: _The Lightning Thief_}{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  and your chosen myth, what value or human fear do both stories reveal?}
  - {SAMPLE-RESPONSE: Based on my comparison of }{SAMPLE-RESPONSE: _The Lightning Thief_}{SAMPLE-RESPONSE:  and }{SAMPLE-RESPONSE: _The Odyssey_}{SAMPLE-RESPONSE: , both stories reveal that people value perseverance when facing uncertainty. Even though Percy and Odysseus are in different worlds, both heroes keep moving through danger instead of giving up.}
- {TEACHER-SCRIPT: How does the hero’s choice define identity in your texts, and where can listeners see that idea in your visual?}
  - {SAMPLE-RESPONSE: The hero’s choice defines identity because both characters become known by what they choose under pressure. In my visual, the arrows point to the decision moments, which helps show that identity is built through action, not just through power.}
- {TEACHER-SCRIPT: How does a modern retelling like }{TEACHER-SCRIPT: _The Lightning Thief_}{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  keep an ancient mythic pattern but change it for modern readers?}
  - {SAMPLE-RESPONSE: Riordan keeps the old mythic pattern of a hero facing supernatural danger, but he changes the setting, voice, and humor for modern readers. That makes the story feel more connected to problems kids might recognize today.}
- {TEACHER-SCRIPT: Why do we still tell stories like these today?}
  - {SAMPLE-RESPONSE: We still tell these stories because people still worry about betrayal, danger, belonging, and identity. Myths keep coming back because those fears and questions have not disappeared.}
- **Say:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  Use your }{TEACHER-SCRIPT: **Reflect and Respond**}{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  graphic organizer for at least two presenters.}
_Display audience expectations and review:_
_**Audience expectations:**_
- Listen actively.
- _Note one strong claim._
- _Note one strong use of evidence or visual explanation._
- _Write specific feedback for at least two classmates._
- Ask follow-up questions respectfully.
_To meet the expectations for Part 3, you should:_
- present a clear claim with evidence
- explain how your visual supports your ideas
- speak clearly using academic language
- paraphrase before responding
- build on others’ ideas using discussion moves
