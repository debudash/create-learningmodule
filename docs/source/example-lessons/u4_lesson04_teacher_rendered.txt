# Lesson 4: The Lightning Thief, Chapter 1  (type: Reading, [50, 50] min)

## SECTION 1: Launch  ([5, 5] min)
[PURPOSE]
  Students will activate prior learning about myth and adaptation and preview how Percy’s perspective and voice modernizes the idea of a hero.
[TABLE]
| H:**Teacher Tip** |
| Before reading, remind students that Greek mythology began as an ancient religion and is being studied here as literature and cultural history. Students do not need to share or adopt any belief system in order to analyze the text. |
| **Teacher Tip** |
| Chapter 1, pp. 1–8, includes outdated and harmful language about disability and mental health as well as negative self-talk about being a “bad kid” or a “nobody.” Name clearly before reading that these words reflect Percy’s limited, hurt perspective and are not acceptable language for the classroom; also remind students that learning differences are real and valid, not explained by fantasy identities. |
[/TABLE]
_Use this routine to connect the Spark lessons to today’s reading. Students should briefly predict what a traditional hero might sound like before they hear Percy’s very different voice and perspective._
_Have students turn to a partner and keep one sentence note in the margin or notes section._
[ROUTINE icon=notes_section] Think-Pair-Share
**Say these Directions:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  Think about the myths and adaptations we have studied so far. First, decide what kind of perspective or point of view you might expect from a traditional hero in ancient myths. Then talk with your partner about how a modern hero’s perspective might be different. Partner A shares first, then Partner B.}
- **Ask:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  Based on our earlier work with myths and adaptation, what kind of point of view would you expect from a traditional hero, and how might Riordan shift that perspective for Percy?}
  - {SAMPLE-RESPONSE: A traditional hero’s point of view might feel distant, formal, or focused on bravery and destiny. Riordan changes that by giving Percy a more personal, relatable perspective—one that shows his confusion, humor, and self‑doubt, making him feel like a real kid instead of a larger‑than‑life figure.}
#### **Connection to Today’s Learning**
- **Say:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  Now we are going to listen closely to Percy’s own words and track how his perspective shapes—and sometimes changes—the kind of hero story we expect.}

## SECTION 2: Literacy Lab  ([10, 10] min)
[PURPOSE]
  Students will be explicitly taught how to embed textual evidence into analytical sentences using noun clauses and precise commentary.
_Today’s language move helps students avoid dropped-in quotations. The goal is to show them how to pull evidence into their own sentences so the analysis sounds smooth and connected._
[ROUTINE icon=notes_section] Language Study
**Say these Directions:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  Effective readers pay attention to how a narrator reveals their perspective through their unique voice. Strong writers take the next step and connect that evidence smoothly to their own ideas. Today, we’re practicing a sentence move that helps us do that: embedding evidence with a noun clause.}
_**Target Sentence Block:**_
- “I was a really bad kid.”
- “I was the only sixth grader who could’ve flunked an intro course to Greek mythology.”
_Display the following Analytical Model Sentence:_
- The fact that Percy calls himself “a really bad kid” reveals that his point of view is defensive and self-critical.
[TABLE]
| H:**Chunk** | H:**Meaning** | H:**Function** |
| The fact that Percy calls himself | This introduces Percy’s words inside my own sentence. | Embeds the evidence smoothly |
| “a really bad kid” | This is the exact text detail I am using. | Provides proof from the text |
| reveals that his point of view comes from a place that is defensive and self-critical | This explains what the detail shows. | Connects evidence to analysis |
[/TABLE]
##### **Teach:**
- **Say:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  When I write about a narrator, I do not want my evidence sitting alone in a separate sentence. I can start with a noun clause like “The fact that . . .” or “Percy’s comment that . . .” to carry the evidence into my analysis. Then I can ask myself what that detail reveals about tone or perspective. This makes my writing sound clear and connected. It also helps the reader see exactly how the evidence supports my idea.}
- **Ask:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  How does the sentence starter “The fact that . . .” help the writer explain Percy’s perspective more clearly?}
  - {SAMPLE-RESPONSE: It helps connect the evidence and the explanation in one sentence. Instead of just dropping in Percy’s words, the writer shows what the detail reveals about his perspective.}
- **Say:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  Complete this sentence frame: “Percy’s comment that he could have flunked Greek mythology suggests that ___.”}
  - {SAMPLE-RESPONSE: Percy’s comment that he could have flunked Greek mythology suggests that he is making fun of himself and does not sound like a proud, traditional hero.}
**Connection to Today’s Learning**
- **Say:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  As we listen to the chapter, you will use the same strategy to explain how Percy’s words shape your first impression of him.}

  [CALLOUT variant=purpose]
    Support students in combining text evidence and analysis into one complete sentence so they can explain perspective more precisely.
    **Language Focus:**
    Noun clauses with the fact that and Percy’s comment that
    Analysis verbs such as reveals, suggests, and shows
    Comparative transitions such as unlike and instead
    Before Activity
    Have students orally rehearse one sentence stem with a partner before writing it.
  [CALLOUT variant=say]
    If students give a quotation without commentary, prompt them to add a reveal word: reveals, suggests, or shows.
    If students can explain their thinking orally but not in writing, have them say the full sentence first and then write exactly what they said.
    “You said Percy is ‘being mean to himself’—we can explain that by saying Percy’s narrator voice is self-critical.”
    “You said he ‘sounds like a regular kid’—we can explain that by saying Riordan gives Percy a modern perspective instead of a formal heroic voice.”
    The fact that Percy says ___ reveals ___.
    Percy’s comment that ___ suggests ___.
    Unlike a traditional hero, Percy sounds ___ because ___.
    Allow students to rehearse their sentence in a shared home language before writing it in English.
    Invite students to compare how they connect evidence in English with how they might explain proof in another language they know.
  [CALLOUT variant=watch]
    If students write the quotation as a separate sentence and stop there → Prompt: “Start again with ‘The fact that . . .’ and finish with what it reveals.”
    If students confuse Percy’s voice with the author’s beliefs → Prompt: “Whose words are these inside the story—Percy’s or Riordan’s? What does Percy’s perspective sound like here?”
    Students combine evidence and explanation in one complete sentence.
    Students use a reveal word to explain perspective instead of only repeating the quotation.

## SECTION 3: Learning in Action  ([30, 30] min)
[PURPOSE]
  **Part A: Analyzing Percy’s Voice and Perspective (RL.6.6, L.6.4.a)**
  Students will listen to a teacher read aloud from Chapter 1, pp. 1–8, and annotate for how Percy’s voice reveals perspective, and to identify myth clues, and examples of adaptation.
  **Part B: Writing Through Percy’s Lens (RL.6.6)**
  Students will explain how Percy’s voice and perspective changes a traditional hero portrayal by using embedded evidence.
### **Part A: Analyzing Percy’s Voice and Perspective (RL.6.6, L.6.4.a) (18 minutes)**
[ROUTINE icon=notes_section] Turn-and-Talk
_Read pp. 1–8 aloud. Prompt students to listen through “Percy’s lens” and annotate for P = perspective , M = myth clue, and A = adaptation._
**Say these Directions:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  As I read aloud, listen to how Percy talks about himself and the world around him, and think about what that reveals about his perspective. Also pay attention to any references to ancient Greek myths and notice whether the language feels adapted for a modern story or closely aligned with the original myth.}
{TEACHER-SCRIPT: Annotate pages 1-8 using the following identifiers: P = perspective , M = myth clue, and A = adaptation. Remember, some moments in the story can connect directly to ancient myths}{TEACHER-SCRIPT: _ and _}{TEACHER-SCRIPT: still be adapted for a modern audience.}
(Display the 3-Column Chart graphic organizer, and have students copy down in their journals.) {TEACHER-SCRIPT: In your 3-Column Chart, record an example from the text of Percy’s narration. Annotate to show what the example reveals about Percy’s perspective, and whether it sounds more like a myth clue, a modern adaptation choice, or both.}
{TEACHER-SCRIPT: Review the following:}
- {TEACHER-SCRIPT: _adaptation_}{TEACHER-SCRIPT: : Adaptation means changing a story’s setting, style, or characters while keeping important themes or patterns.}
- {TEACHER-SCRIPT: _perspective_}{TEACHER-SCRIPT: : the unique outlook, attitude, or "lens" through which a character or narrator views the events, characters, and world within a story}
- {TEACHER-SCRIPT: _myth clue_}{TEACHER-SCRIPT: : a word or phrase in the text that connects to an ancient myth}
- **Say:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  As I read, I’m listening for what Percy says and what those details reveal about his perspective—how he sees himself and his world.}
{TEACHER-SCRIPT: When he warns the reader about being a half‑blood, his blunt, joking tone shows he doesn’t view himself like a traditional hero.}
{TEACHER-SCRIPT: When he calls himself a “bad kid,” it tells me how he’s been labeled by others and how he might see himself, which seems very different from how an ancient Greek hero might portray himself. And when myth‑related words appear, I think about how Riordan blends everyday school life with ancient Greek ideas.}
_During the read-aloud, pause briefly at these moments in the text:_
- The opening warning to the reader about being a half-blood
- The section where Percy calls himself “a really bad kid”
- The section where Percy mentions being expelled
- The classroom moment where Percy hears the word _immortal_
- **Ask:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  At the opening where Percy warns the reader away from his life, what does he reveal about his perspective, and how is that different from a traditional hero perspective?}
  - {SAMPLE-RESPONSE: Percy sounds blunt and sarcastic right away. Instead of sounding proud or noble like a traditional hero, he sounds like a kid who is tired of his own problems and is almost daring the reader to leave. The text states, “If you’re reading this because you think you might be one, my advice is: close this book right now.”}
- **Ask:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  In the section where Percy describes himself as “a really bad kid” and mentions being expelled, what do we learn about how he sees himself?}
  - {SAMPLE-RESPONSE: We learn that Percy has heard negative labels so often that he repeats them. His perspective seems defensive because he explains his problems before anyone else can judge him. The text states, “Look, I didn’t want to be a half-blood.”}
- **Ask:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  In the classroom section about the gods, what context clues help you infer the meaning of }{TEACHER-SCRIPT: _immortal_}{TEACHER-SCRIPT: ?}
  - {SAMPLE-RESPONSE: The text around the word talks about gods and death, so it suggests that immortal means not dying or living forever. The root mort also connects to death, which helps confirm that meaning.}
[COLLAPSIBLE "Integrated ELD Instructions"]
  [CALLOUT variant=purpose]
    Support students in annotating how word choice and narration communicate perspective, mythic elements, cultural beliefs, and Riordan’s adaptation choices.
    **Language Focus:**
    Annotation language with voice, myth clue, adaptation, and perspective
    Explanation frames using because, this suggests, and this reveals
    Comparison language using both, unlike, and however
    Before Activity
    Quickly rehearse the three annotation codes: V for voice, M for myth clue, and A for adaptation.
  [CALLOUT variant=say]
    Prompt students to name the exact word or phrase Percy uses before they explain what it reveals.
    When students notice myth references, ask one more question: “Is this acting like an old myth element, or is Riordan changing it for a modern school setting?”
    “You said, ‘This sounds like mythology class’—we can say, ‘This is a myth clue because it introduces Greek gods into Percy’s school world.’”
    “You said, ‘He jokes about it’—we can say, ‘Percy’s sarcastic word choice makes the tone sound sharp and modern.’”
    This detail reveals Percy’s perspective because ___.
    This is a myth clue because ___.
    Unlike a traditional hero opening, Percy’s narration ___.
    Encourage students to use symbols, abbreviations, or home-language notes during annotation if that helps them capture their thinking quickly.
    Invite students to compare Percy’s sarcastic tone to storytelling voices they know from other texts, oral traditions, or media.
  [CALLOUT variant=watch]
    If students identify a funny or sharp line but cannot explain its effect → Prompt: “What feeling does that line create for the reader—admiration, distance, humor, frustration, or something else?”
    If students confuse a myth reference with a full myth retelling → Prompt: “Is Riordan retelling the myth here, or is he planting a clue from myth inside Percy’s modern life?”
    Students name a specific word or text detail before making an inference.
    Students distinguish between a myth clue and a modern adaptation choice instead of treating them as the same thing.
[/COLLAPSIBLE]
**Connection to Today’s Learning**
- **Say:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  Now that you have heard Percy’s voice and collected evidence, you are ready to explain how that voice changes the idea of a hero.}

### **Part B: Writing Through Percy’s Lens (RL.6.6) (12 minutes)**
[ROUTINE icon=writing] Quick Write
_Students now use the evidence they collected to answer the lesson TDQ. Push them to use one embedded quotation or paraphrase and one comparison to a traditional hero._
_Display the following writing model if needed for support and guidance._
- **Say:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  The fact that Percy introduces himself with a warning instead of a proud hero speech changes the kind of hero we expect. Percy’s comment that he is “a really bad kid” makes him sound defensive and sarcastic, not noble or perfect. Unlike a traditional hero, who often seems confident from the start, Percy sounds like someone who has been misunderstood for a long time. This perspective makes him feel more modern and more real to the reader.}
**Say these Directions:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  Use your notes from the read-aloud to write a three- to four-sentence response to today’s question. Be sure to use one noun clause starter, such as “The fact that . . .” or “Percy’s comment that . . . ,” and at least one comparative word such as }{TEACHER-SCRIPT: _unlike_}{TEACHER-SCRIPT: , }{TEACHER-SCRIPT: _however_}{TEACHER-SCRIPT: , or }{TEACHER-SCRIPT: _instead_}{TEACHER-SCRIPT: .}
- **Ask:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  How does Percy’s self-talk change the way a “hero” is traditionally portrayed?}
  - {SAMPLE-RESPONSE: The fact that Percy talks about himself like he is already in trouble changes the way a hero is portrayed. Percy’s comment that he is “a really bad kid” makes him sound sarcastic and defensive. Unlike a traditional hero, who might seem brave and certain right away, Percy sounds like a real kid who expects people to judge him. That makes the reader meet him as a modern hero, not a perfect one.}
[COLLAPSIBLE "Integrated ELD Instructions"]
  [CALLOUT variant=purpose]
    Support students in writing complete analytical sentences that connect evidence, comparison, and explanation.
    **Language Focus:**
    Noun clause starters for embedding evidence
    Comparison transitions
    Explanation language for point of view and tone
    Before Activity
    Have students underline their chosen evidence in their notes and orally rehearse one sentence before writing.
  [CALLOUT variant=say]
    If a student has only one sentence, prompt for a second sentence that compares Percy to a traditional hero.
    If a student uses evidence without analysis, ask, “What does that detail make the reader think about Percy?”
    “You said, ‘Heroes are usually serious’—we can say, ‘Unlike a traditional hero, Percy’s narration sounds sarcastic and informal.’”
    “You said, ‘This shows he feels judged’—we can say, ‘Percy’s perspective reveals that he expects other people to see him as a problem kid.’”
    The fact that Percy ___ reveals that ___.
    Percy’s comment that ___ changes the hero portrayal because ___.
    Unlike a traditional hero, Percy ___.
    Allow students to talk through their full response with a partner before writing.
    Validate concise, clear language first, then help students expand it into a more academic sentence structure.
  [CALLOUT variant=watch]
    If students list two details but do not connect them → Prompt: “Use because, unlike, or this reveals to show the relationship between the details and your idea.”
    If students overgeneralize with “heroes are always perfect” → Prompt: “Be more precise. Say ‘many traditional heroes’ or ‘the kind of hero we often expect.’”
    Students use one embedded piece of evidence to explain Percy’s perspective.
    Students compare Percy to a traditional hero with a precise transition instead of only retelling the opening.
