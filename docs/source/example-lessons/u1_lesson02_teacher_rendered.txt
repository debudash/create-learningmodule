# Lesson 2: Ordinary Moments, Extraordinary Stories, Part 2  (type: Spark, [50, 50] min)

## SECTION 1: Launch  ([15, 15] min)
[PURPOSE]
  Students will discuss ordinary moments that made them feel seen and taught them about a friend or family member.
[ROUTINE icon=notes_section] Think-Pair-Share
_Have students review the previous lesson’s homework and share their answers to the question of why Jason Reynolds writes stories._
- **Ask:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  Based on what you read, why does Jason Reynolds write stories?}
_Pause for students to discuss._
**Say these Directions:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  Think about an ordinary moment that made you feel seen and understood. Then think about an ordinary moment that taught you something about a friend or family member. }{TEACHER-SCRIPT: **Think-Pair-Share**}{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  responses with a classmate.}
- {SAMPLE-RESPONSE: I snapped at my sister when I was really tired, and she didn’t snap back. Instead, she asked if I was OK and suggested I take a nap. I felt seen. The way my mom handled being late for work taught me that she is patient and not too hard on herself when she makes a mistake.}
#### **Connection to Today’s Learning**
- **Say:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  Jason Reynolds writes stories to help young people see themselves. Feeling seen and understood is important because it helps us feel a sense of community and belonging. Today, we’ll read an article about Jason Reynolds.}

## SECTION 2: Learning in Action  ([30, 30] min)
[PURPOSE]
  **Part A: “Writing the Truth: Jason Reynolds and the Power of Being Seen” (RI.6.4)**
  Students will read the article in class and discuss multiple-meaning words and the impact they have on meaning.
  **Part B: Give One, Get One (RI.6.4, SL.6.1a-c)**
  Students will participate in Give One, Get One to share ideas about the article.
### **Part A: “Writing the Truth: Jason Reynolds and the Power of Being Seen” (RI.6.4) (15 minutes)**
_This section introduces students to how authors use word choice to shape meaning. The focus is not just identifying literal and figurative meanings, but understanding how those choices help communicate the author’s message about identity and belonging. Consider modeling one clear example and guide students to connect word meaning to the author's purpose._
[TABLE]
| H:**Teacher Tip** |
| Set the expectation that all students will not only identify literal and figurative meanings but also explain how word choice shapes meaning and message. This skill builds across the unit and prepares students for future analysis and writing tasks where they must use precise language and explain how authors develop ideas. |
[/TABLE]
[TABLE]
| H:**Teacher Tip** |
| “Writing the Truth: Jason Reynolds and the Power of Being Seen” introduces Jason Reynolds as an author who writes about young people’s real experiences, voices, and communities. Use this context to help students understand why the article emphasizes words such as _seen_, _voice_, _mirror_, and _center_. These word choices connect to Reynolds’s larger purpose: helping readers recognize themselves and others in stories. There are no illustrations central to this selection. |

_Read “Writing the Truth: Jason Reynolds and the Power of Being Seen” as a large group, pausing to check comprehension as you read._
##### **Teach: Class Read**
**Say these Directions:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  As we read “Writing the Truth: Jason Reynolds and the Power of Being Seen,}{TEACHER-SCRIPT: _”_}{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  look for words that have differing meanings. Think about how the author’s word choices help readers understand the author’s message.}
_Guide students in understanding the difference between literal and figurative meanings._
- **Say:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  When someone speaks literally, they are giving you the dictionary definition of the words. There are no hidden messages, no exaggerations, and no decorations. It’s just the facts. For example: ‘It is raining.’ Figurative language uses creative comparisons to describe a feeling or an idea. You aren't supposed to take these words at face value. Instead, the author is using "figures of speech" to make their writing more colorful or emotional. For example: ‘The sky cried tears of joy.’}
{TEACHER-SCRIPT: Throughout this lesson, use the graphic organizer to keep track of the literal and figurative meanings of words. Label the columns as follows:}
- Column 1: Word
- Column 2: Literal Meaning
- Column 3: Figurative Meaning
_Lead the class in an analysis of the article that focuses on word meanings and how the author’s use of these words impacts the reader’s experience._
- **Say:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  In this article, Reynolds shares how the power of stories can be }{TEACHER-SCRIPT: _transformative_}{TEACHER-SCRIPT: . When something is transformative, it causes a lasting and significant change. A person won’t transform in the literal sense, like an everyday person becoming a superhero. But figuratively, they are different.}
- **Say:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  Another example is the word }{TEACHER-SCRIPT: _reflect_}{TEACHER-SCRIPT: . Literally, }{TEACHER-SCRIPT: _reflect_}{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  means to bounce light back, like a mirror. Figuratively, it means to represent—or mirror—someone’s life experiences. This matters because the author wants readers to understand that books can represent real lives.}
- **Ask:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  What are some other examples of words that have literal and figurative meanings?}
  - {SAMPLE-RESPONSE: bright, cold, heavy, sharp}
[COLLAPSIBLE "Integrated ELD Instructions"]
  [CALLOUT variant=purpose]
    Support students in analyzing the meanings of words by distinguishing literal vs. figurative meaning and explaining how the author’s choice affects the reader.
  [CALLOUT variant=say]
    Require students to justify figurative meaning with a sentence clue.
    Model one full example from the article before students add their own.
    Add academic verbs (reveals, suggests, emphasizes, implies).
    Turn “It means . . . ” into “In this context, it suggests . . . ”
    Connect word choice to effect: “This helps readers ___.”
    Literally, ___ means ___.
    Figuratively, ___ suggests ___.
    The author uses ___ to show/reveal ___.
    This matters because it helps the reader ___.
  [CALLOUT variant=watch]
    Leverage cognates and multilingual knowledge when relevant (reflect/reflectar; transform/transformar) while still requiring evidence from the sentence.
    Encourage students to use synonyms to test meaning (mirror → reflect → show).
    Anchor the 3-Column Chart routine: word → literal meaning → figurative meaning in THIS sentence.
    If figurative meaning is too literal → Prompt: What idea is the author trying to show beyond the dictionary meaning?
    If students list meanings without effect → Prompt: Add “ This matters because . . . ”
    Students correctly separate literal vs. figurative.
    Students cite a phrase from the sentence as evidence.
    Students explain the effect on meaning or reader understanding.
[/COLLAPSIBLE]

### **Part B: Give One, Get One (RI.6.4, SL.6.1a-c) **(15 minutes)
_This collaborative routine helps students deepen their understanding of the article by exchanging and refining ideas. The goal is for students to connect figurative language (e.g., “see,” “mirror”) to Reynolds’s purpose and explain how stories help people feel understood. Emphasize idea-building, not just idea-sharing._
[ROUTINE icon=graphic_organizer] Give One, Get One
_After students have read the article, complete a few rounds of __**Give One, Get One**__ using the following questions as guides._
**Say these Directions:**{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  Circulate and share an idea about the article you read with one classmate at a time. After you share, offer feedback on your classmate’s idea, using the following sentence frame if needed: “One idea I gave was ____. One idea I got was ____.” Jot down both your ideas and those you hear from classmates in the }{TEACHER-SCRIPT: **Give One, Get One**}{TEACHER-SCRIPT:  graphic organizer.}
- {TEACHER-SCRIPT: What does it mean for an artist to “see” young people?}
  - {SAMPLE-RESPONSE: It means the artist can relate to young people or imagine what it would feel like to be one.}
- {TEACHER-SCRIPT: How can stories show what matters to us?}
  - {SAMPLE-RESPONSE: If a story deals with a situation that I care about, then it will matter to me. If a story is about something that happens in my life, it will matter to me.}
- {TEACHER-SCRIPT: What has to happen to make an ordinary moment worth sharing?}
  - {SAMPLE-RESPONSE: The moment just has to mean something to someone in some way. It has to be important, at least a little bit, or different from usual.}
_This activity reinforces both speaking/listening skills and conceptual understanding of how language shapes meaning. Listen for students making connections between figurative language and the article’s message, and use this as a checkpoint before moving to reflection._
[COLLAPSIBLE "Integrated ELD Instructions"]
  [CALLOUT variant=purpose]
    Help students share and refine ideas in Give One, Get One by using academic talk moves, referencing the article’s message, and using figurative meanings of key words (see, mirror, center).
  [CALLOUT variant=say]
    Encourage students who are quieter to “give” first in writing, then say it aloud.
    Require each student to “give” one idea tied to the text and “get” one idea they can restate.
    Prompt students to connect answers back to Reynolds’s purpose (young people seeing themselves).
    Encourage respectful follow-ups (“What makes you say that?”).
    Expand answers with an example (a story detail, a reader feeling).
    Use reasoning connectors: because, so, which shows.
    One idea I gave was ___ because ___.
    One idea I got was ___, and it made me think ___.
    Reynolds suggests ___ when he says ___.
    This connects to the article because ___.
  [CALLOUT variant=watch]
    Allow students to start with lived experience, then require a bridge to the text (“This relates because . . . ”).
    Add precision: “see young people” → “notice their real experiences/voice.”
    If students answer off-text → Prompt: Add one connection to the article’s message.
    If students use literal meaning only (“see = look”) → Prompt: Use the figurative meaning (see = understand/recognize).
    Students share one idea and restate one peer’s idea accurately.
    Students use at least one academic verb (suggests , reveals).
    Students make one explicit link to the article’s purpose or message.
[/COLLAPSIBLE]
